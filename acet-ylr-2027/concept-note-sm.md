# Adoption Is Not Transformation: Green Technology and Value Addition in Ghana's Food Processing Micro-Enterprises

**Thematic area:** Green Industrial Transformation

## Background

Green industrial policy in Africa is financed on the basis of units deployed. Results-based financing pays per verified device installed, and national plans report units disseminated. Such figures record procurement, not production. Green industrialisation requires that equipment be used to make something of higher value. If much of what is deployed never enters production, these instruments are conditioned on the wrong measure.

## Framing and evidence

The problem is an energy efficiency gap among informal firms. Allcott and Greenstone (2012) explain why firms forgo privately profitable energy investments; audited European enterprises adopt only 40 to 48 per cent of recommended measures. The pattern recurs in low-income settings, where 75 per cent of connected enterprises in electrified Indian villages draw under one kilowatt-hour daily (Pelz et al. 2021). Yet the mechanism does operate where identification is strong (Dinkelman 2011).

A second literature reaches a related conclusion about capital. Fafchamps et al. (2013) find that in urban Ghana, women running subsistence enterprises gained nothing from cash or in-kind grants, while larger women-owned firms grew only when capital arrived in kind. Neither literature has examined what happens when that capital is itself the green technology.

## Research question

Conditional on adoption, what converts green processing technology into value addition, and which complementary input is binding?

Two hypotheses follow from evidence rather than speculation. Conversion should be non-linear in enterprise scale, and probably inverted: small processors lack the working capital to buy fish to smoke, while large processors find the improved oven's capacity too low, so conversion should peak in the middle. Second, following Field et al. (2019), conversion should be weaker where the household contains a second enterprise, because returns are redirected towards it.

## Setting and data

The setting is coastal fish smoking, where women are 97 per cent of processors. Traditional Chorkor ovens deposit polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at 298 micrograms per kilogramme against a European Union limit of 12, while improved Ahotor ovens reach 59. The technology therefore closes roughly 80 per cent of the distance to compliance and still leaves output five times over the limit, which makes the certification pathway a question rather than an assumption. Monitoring of the first Ahotor cohort found that 36 per cent of recipients had never used the oven, that 64 per cent still smoked on the Chorkor, and that some used the new oven only for household consumption. Enterprise outcomes have never been measured. Inland cassava and shea processing supply contrast and the scale variation the first hypothesis needs.

The sample is 500 enterprises, roughly 300 adopters and 200 non-adopters from the same clusters, drawn from programme and distributor records carrying the installation dates the event study requires, and from enumeration at landing sites.

## Identification and measurement

Three strategies are combined. A retrospective panel supports an event study with enterprise fixed effects around adoption timing. Comparing converters with non-converters holds adoption constant, removing selection into adoption while remaining diagnostic rather than causal. A discrete choice experiment randomises support packages across capital form, training, supervision, after-sales service and certification linkage, with attributes and price ranges calibrated in formative interviews. Three hundred adopters completing eight tasks yield 2,400 observations for mixed logit.

Two measurement decisions follow from the evidence. Profit is elicited directly, since detailed revenue and expense questions are less accurate and firms underreport revenues by roughly 30 per cent (de Mel et al. 2009). Use is verified by enumerator inspection, since self-reports overstate use by 28 to 46 per cent and miss disadoption (Hing et al. 2023). The gap between reported and verified use is itself a result.

## Outcomes

The primary outcome is enterprise profit per hour of processing labour. Secondary outcomes are verified use, share of batches smoked on each oven, buyer type, paid employment, spoilage, fuelwood cost per pan processed, and women's control over revenue. Energy is measured as intensity, not level, since successful conversion expands output and total fuel spending may rise. With 165 converters and 135 non-converters, the design detects 0.33 standard deviations at 80 per cent power.

## Contribution and policy relevance

The study produces the first verified conversion rate for green processing technology in Africa, with an experimental ranking of what closes the gap. Programme monitoring has recorded non-use but never what it costs in output or income. The nearest studies estimate adoption-earnings associations in Nigeria (Owusu-Sekyere et al. 2024) and Kenya (Duke and Shell Foundation 2025), but neither measures conversion nor identifies the binding constraint.

Green industrial support is meant to be conditional and performance-based. Results-based financing conditions on deployment because conversion has never been measured. This study makes it measurable.
