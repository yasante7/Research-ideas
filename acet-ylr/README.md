## What ACET is actually buying

ACET is a policy institute, not a research council. It funds work that a finance ministry or trade ministry could act on inside a year. Every idea below is built to the same five-part shape, and you should keep that shape in the concept note:

1. **A policy that exists and is contested** (a levy, a ban, a set-aside, a border regime) — not a general topic.
2. **An identification strategy** — something that changed at a date, at a border, for one group and not another.
3. **Data you can actually get for $15k** — administrative records, trade mirror statistics, scraped portals, or a small targeted survey. Not a nationally representative panel.
4. **One number a minister needs** — a cost, a leakage rate, a pass-through elasticity, an exposure share.
5. **A named audience** — the ministry, the regulator, the secretariat that will receive the brief.

Fit to ACET's *Growth with DEPTH* framing (Diversification, Export competitiveness, Productivity, Technology upgrading, Human well-being) explicitly, in one sentence, in the concept note.

## Tier 1 — strongest four (highest fundability per dollar)

### 1. CBAM exposure: what the EU carbon border levy costs African exporters
**Theme:** Green Industrial Transformation

**Question:** Which African export lines are exposed to the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism now that the definitive regime has begun, how large is the implied cost as a share of export value, and which exposures are avoidable through measurement rather than decarbonisation?

**Why it wins:** African governments are negotiating on CBAM with almost no continent-specific numbers of their own. Aluminium, cement, fertiliser, iron/steel, and electricity exposure is concentrated in a handful of countries (Mozambique, South Africa, Egypt, Ghana, Cameroon, Zimbabwe). The default-value penalty — exporters who cannot document their emissions intensity get assigned punitive defaults — means a share of the cost is an *information* problem, which is a much more tractable policy recommendation than "decarbonise."

**Method:** Product-level exposure mapping. UN Comtrade / ITC Trade Map export flows at HS6 to the EU, matched to the CBAM product scope; emissions intensities from plant-level disclosures, IEA/IRENA grid factors, and national energy balances. Compute (a) exposed export value by country and product, (b) implied levy at a range of ETS prices, (c) the gap between actual and default-value liability. Then 10–15 semi-structured interviews with exporters and one export-promotion agency on reporting readiness.

**Output:** An "African CBAM exposure index" with a public dataset, plus a policy brief on a low-cost national MRV (monitoring, reporting, verification) facility.

**Budget fit:** Excellent. Mostly desk work; one country visit for interviews. ~$15k comfortably.

> **Deep dive:** `cbam-brief.md` covers what CBAM is, the regulatory timeline, the problem in three layers, the verified research gap, a draft problem statement, and the funding justification with a method-matched budget.

### 2. Did the raw-lithium export ban build a value chain, or just move the rents?
**Theme:** Green Industrial Transformation

**Question:** After Zimbabwe's 2022 ban on raw lithium exports (and comparable beneficiation mandates elsewhere), did domestic value added, employment, and fiscal revenue rise — or did trade simply reroute through concentrate re-classification and neighbouring ports?

**Why it wins:** Half the continent is drafting critical-minerals local-content law right now on the strength of an untested assumption. A clean before/after with a credible comparison group is genuinely decision-relevant, and the mirror-statistics trick (comparing reported exports against partners' reported imports) is a cheap, defensible way to detect rerouting and misclassification.

**Method:** Difference-in-differences on HS-code-level trade values and unit values, treated = banned mineral-country pairs, control = unbanned minerals in the same country and the same mineral in unbanned countries. Mirror-statistics gap analysis for leakage. Complement with company filings, mining-cadastre data, and 8–12 interviews with regulators and processors.

**Output:** A "what beneficiation mandates deliver" evidence note for the African Minerals Development Centre and national mines ministries; a design checklist distinguishing mandates that worked from those that displaced trade.

**Budget fit:** Very good. Trade data is free; the cost is fieldwork and a research assistant.

### 3. The e-levy post-mortem: what a repealed mobile-money tax teaches about taxing digital payments
**Theme:** Innovation & Digital Policy

**Question:** Ghana introduced an electronic transfer levy in 2022 and repealed it in 2025. Did transaction volumes and values recover fully after repeal, or did the episode leave a persistent behavioural scar — cash reversion, transaction splitting, account dormancy — and who bore it?

**Why it wins:** This is a rare policy round-trip: imposition *and* removal, with high-frequency administrative data on both sides. Uganda, Cameroon, Tanzania, and Zimbabwe still run similar levies and are all revisiting them. The hysteresis question — whether digital-payment adoption is permanently damaged by a tax that was later removed — has essentially no evidence behind it anywhere, and it is exactly the sort of thing a finance ministry weighing a new levy needs.

**Method:** Interrupted time series on central bank / switch-operator aggregate mobile money statistics (volume, value, average transaction size, active accounts), with the imposition and repeal as two breaks. Synthetic control using comparable non-treated markets. Bunching analysis around the exemption threshold to detect transaction splitting. A merchant/agent survey (n≈300, one region) on cash reversion among low-value users.

**Output:** A design note on how to tax digital finance without killing adoption — thresholds, exemptions, and what recovery after repeal actually looks like.

**Budget fit:** Good. Main costs are the survey and data access fees.

### 4. Does the 30% women-and-youth procurement set-aside reach women-owned firms?
**Theme:** Gender Equality

**Question:** Under preferential public-procurement regimes (Kenya's AGPO 30% reservation and its analogues), what share of reserved contract value actually goes to genuinely women-owned firms, how concentrated are the awards, and do winners survive and grow?

**Why it wins:** Set-asides are the most widely copied gender-economic policy on the continent and among the least audited. "Fronting" — a woman-registered shell for a male-owned business — is universally alleged and almost never measured. Award data is public on tender portals, which makes this a rare gender-equality question answerable with hard administrative data rather than another perceptions survey.

**Method:** Scrape award notices from the national procurement portal(s) over 5+ years; match to the company registry for ownership, directorship, and incorporation date. Measure concentration (share of value to the top 10 suppliers), and the incidence of firms incorporated shortly before certification, sharing addresses/directors with non-reserved suppliers, or bidding in both reserved and open lots. Follow-up survey of 60–80 certified suppliers on payment delays and working-capital access — usually the real binding constraint.

**Output:** A leakage estimate plus a concrete fix list: verification triggers, payment-term guarantees, unbundling of large lots.

**Budget fit:** Very good. Scraping plus a small survey; a data-scraping RA is the main line item.

## Tier 2 — six more, ready to develop

### 5. Preference utilisation under AfCFTA: the paperwork price of a tariff cut
**Theme:** Regional Integration
Why do so few consignments claim AfCFTA preferences? Measure the per-shipment administrative cost of a certificate of origin (fees, days, agent charges) from customs (ASYCUDA) records and an exporter survey, then estimate the preference-margin threshold below which claiming is not worth the trouble. Delivers a preference-utilisation rate — a number most member states cannot currently produce — and a rules-of-origin simplification argument grounded in cost, not principle.

### 6. PAPSS and the cost of paying across an African border
**Theme:** Regional Integration
Does settling intra-African trade payments in local currency through the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System actually beat correspondent banking in dollars? A mystery-shopping audit of quoted fees and FX spreads across 6–8 currency corridors and 20+ banks, repeated over several months, plus bank interviews on why adoption lags. Produces the first independent corridor-level cost series for PAPSS and a direct estimate of the dollar-intermediation tax on African trade.

### 7. Generative AI exposure of African jobs — and of the BPO growth strategy
**Theme:** Innovation & Digital Policy
Several governments are betting on outsourcing and digital services for youth employment. Map task-level AI exposure indices onto occupation codes in national labour force surveys to estimate exposed employment shares by country, gender, and education level, with a focused look at the BPO and freelancing sector. Almost pure secondary data — cheap, fast, highly citable, and directly challenges or supports a live industrial-policy bet.

### 8. Digital ID, e-KYC, and credit for thin-file borrowers
**Theme:** Innovation & Digital Policy
Did linking national digital identity to bank and lender KYC expand credit access to borrowers without formal credit histories — and at what interest rates? Exploit staggered enrolment or mandate deadlines in a difference-in-differences on credit bureau aggregates and lender-level data, disaggregated by gender. Tests the core promise of digital public infrastructure: that identity begets inclusion.

### 9. Non-tariff barriers on a staple-food corridor
**Theme:** Regional Integration
Take one high-volume corridor (Aflao–Noepé, Malaba, Beitbridge, Busia). Combine truck transit times, roadblock/informal-payment diaries kept by drivers, and market price series on both sides to estimate how much of the border cost passes through to consumer staple prices. Converts an abstract "trade facilitation" agenda into a price-per-kilo number and gives corridor authorities a monitoring instrument they can rerun.

### 10. Simplified Trade Regimes and women in informal cross-border trade
**Theme:** Gender Equality / Regional Integration
Simplified Trade Regimes exist to let small traders — overwhelmingly women — clear goods without a customs broker. Do the traders know they exist, do they qualify, and what happens to their costs and harassment exposure when they use them? Border-post intercept survey (n≈400) at two posts, with a randomised information treatment on eligibility to test whether awareness alone shifts uptake. Strong on both gender and regional-integration criteria, and the information experiment gives it causal teeth on a small budget.

## Choosing between them

| If your situation is… | Pitch |
|---|---|
| Strong quantitative/econometrics, limited fieldwork budget or travel constraints | **1 (CBAM)**, **2 (lithium ban)**, or **7 (AI exposure)** — all essentially desk-based |
| You have institutional access to a central bank, customs, or a regulator | **3 (e-levy)**, **5 (AfCFTA preferences)**, or **8 (digital ID)** — access is the moat |
| You can run enumerators and want fieldwork | **4 (procurement)**, **9 (corridor)**, or **10 (STR)** |
| You want the cleanest causal identification | **2**, **3**, or **10** — each has a real discontinuity or randomisation |
| You want the widest policy audience | **1** and **6** — continental questions, few incumbents |

Do not pitch two. The concept note is 800 words; a hedged application reads as an undecided researcher.

## Concept note skeleton (800 words, hard cap)

| Section | Words | What must be in it |
|---|---|---|
| Title + theme | 20 | Name the ACET theme explicitly |
| The policy problem | 120 | The specific policy, who is debating it, why the evidence gap is binding *now* |
| Research questions | 80 | One primary question, two subsidiary. Each answerable with the data you name below |
| Data and methodology | 250 | Name the datasets and how you will obtain them. Name the identification strategy. State the sample size. This is where applications are won or lost |
| Expected contribution | 150 | The specific number(s) you will produce, the output (paper + brief + dataset), and the named institution that receives it |
| Feasibility and leadership | 120 | Your access, prior work, institutional affiliation, and why *you* specifically can execute this in 12 months |
| Risks | 60 | Two real risks and your mitigation. Including this signals seriousness — omitting it signals inexperience |

**Common failure modes to avoid:** a topic instead of a question; "mixed methods" with no identification strategy; a data plan that requires a survey you cannot afford; no named policy audience; and a budget that quietly implies a team of five.
