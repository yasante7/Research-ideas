# CBAM: what it is, why it is a research problem, and why it needs funding
### Background brief for an ACET YLR concept note — Green Industrial Transformation

---

## 1. What CBAM actually is

The **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism** is an EU import charge on the greenhouse gas emissions embedded in certain goods. It is not a tariff on the product; it is a charge on the *carbon content* of the product.

**The logic.** EU producers of cement, steel, aluminium and fertiliser pay for their emissions through the EU Emissions Trading System. Historically they were shielded by free ETS allowances, on the argument that otherwise production would relocate to jurisdictions without carbon pricing ("carbon leakage"). The EU is now phasing out those free allowances. CBAM is the replacement shield: as domestic producers start paying the full ETS price, importers are made to pay an equivalent price at the border. Import charge in, free allowances out, in lockstep.

**Covered sectors:** cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, and hydrogen, plus a set of downstream products (screws, bolts and similar steel/aluminium articles). Scope extension to further downstream goods is under review.

**How the charge is computed, in four steps:**

1. **Embedded emissions** of the imported good are determined — tonnes CO₂e per tonne of product.
2. Multiplied by the **CBAM certificate price**, which tracks the weekly average EU ETS auction price. The importer buys and surrenders certificates.
3. **Minus any carbon price already paid in the country of origin.** A domestic carbon tax or ETS is creditable. Fuel excise taxes, emission standards, and regulatory abatement are **not** — a point of live legal contention [7].
4. Multiplied by a **phase-in factor** that rises over time as EU free allocation is withdrawn, reaching full application in the mid-2030s. *(Take the exact annual schedule from the consolidated Regulation — it was amended in 2025 and I would not rely on any secondary source for it, including this one.)*

**The part that matters most for Africa — step 1.** An exporter who can produce verified installation-level emissions data is charged on its actual emissions. An exporter who cannot is assigned a **default value**, deliberately set conservatively — a worst-case proxy rather than a neutral average. The design intent is explicit: the default is a sanction that pressures exporters into verified reporting. One legal analysis reads CBAM as a two-tier climate club, where tier one prices verified emissions and tier two prices data absence, and notes that monitoring, reporting and verification capacity is scarcest precisely in lower-income jurisdictions, so their exporters are the most likely to be pushed onto the punitive tier [4].

That is the crux. **Part of Africa's CBAM bill is a carbon problem. Part of it is a paperwork problem.** They have completely different policy remedies, and nobody has measured the split.

## 2. When it was introduced — the timeline that makes this urgent

| Date | What happened |
|---|---|
| July 2021 | Proposed by the European Commission under the Fit for 55 package |
| **May 2023** | **Regulation (EU) 2023/956 adopted** — CBAM becomes law |
| 1 Oct 2023 – 31 Dec 2025 | **Transitional phase.** Quarterly reporting of embedded emissions by importers. No money changes hands. Generates the first real dataset on who imports what and with what emissions intensity |
| 8 Oct 2025 | **Omnibus simplification Regulation adopted**; in force 20 Oct 2025. Introduces a 50-tonne-per-importer-per-year mass-based de minimis exemption, removes the old €150 value threshold, and defers certificate surrender |
| **1 Jan 2026** | **Definitive regime begins.** Imports from this date carry a real financial liability |
| 1 Feb 2027 | Sale of CBAM certificates begins, covering goods imported during 2026 |
| From 2027 | Declarants must hold certificates covering at least 50% of embedded emissions to date at each quarter end |
| **30 Sep 2027** | **First annual CBAM declaration and certificate surrender**, for calendar-year 2026 imports |

Read the last three rows against the YLR grant calendar. A twelve-month project running through 2027 sits **exactly on top of the first real liability cycle in CBAM's history** — the first certificate purchases, the first quarterly holding requirements, the first annual declarations. Fieldwork during that window catches exporters in the act of deciding whether to invest in verified emissions data or eat the default. That is a one-time observational opportunity and it does not repeat.

*Verify every date above against the consolidated Regulation before you cite it. The Omnibus moved several of them and secondary sources are inconsistent.*

## 3. The problem, in three layers

### Layer 1 — Exposure is small for Africa as a whole and existential for a few economies

The continental aggregate is modest, which is exactly why the issue gets under-prioritised in continental fora. The country-level concentration is severe. Published estimates put Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Cameroon among the most exposed economies globally in relative terms, with Morocco added when socio-economic exposure is measured [1]; the World Bank's relative exposure index puts Zimbabwe and Mozambique alongside Ukraine, Georgia and India at the top. Mozambique is the standard illustration: aluminium dominates its export basket, the overwhelming majority of that aluminium goes to the EU, and its export carbon intensity is roughly an order of magnitude above the EU average. Estimates of the export decline under full pricing run high enough to constitute a macroeconomic event, not a trade irritant.

Note the estimates disagree with each other — one source puts CBAM goods at 18.9% of Mozambique's exports, another at 13%, against roughly 2% for South Africa. **The disagreement is itself a finding**: definitions of the exposed basket differ, and no one has produced a reconciled, product-level exposure measure that African negotiators can actually stand behind.

### Layer 2 — The information penalty nobody has priced

If an exporter cannot document its emissions, it pays the default. For a hydro-powered aluminium smelter — and several African smelters run substantially on hydro — the actual emissions intensity may be *far below* the default it gets assigned. That exporter is not being charged for its carbon. It is being charged for its **inability to prove its carbon**, and it may be paying more than a dirtier European competitor.

This is theorised [4] and appears as an input to risk indices [8], but as a measured quantity for African exporters it does not exist. The one firm-level MRV readiness instrument I could find was piloted on **four anonymised Egyptian fertiliser firms** [Maghawry et al. 2025] — a diagnostic tool, not an estimate.

Why this layer matters more than the headline exposure number: "decarbonise your grid" is a decade-long, capital-intensive answer. "Build a national MRV facility so your exporters get charged their real emissions" is a two-year, low-cost answer — **if** the gap between actual and default is large enough to pay for it. That is an empirical question with a computable answer and no one has computed it.

### Layer 3 — Every published African exposure estimate is now out of date

This is the sharpest point available to you, and it is a gift of timing.

The quantitative CBAM–Africa literature is uniformly **ex-ante simulation**: multi-regional input-output analysis [1], input-output inequality assessment [2], GTAP computable general equilibrium [3], MRIO competitiveness modelling [6], dynamic CGE [9], CGE for EU–Africa agriculture [10], gravity-plus-CGE for six developing exporters [5]. A 2025 systematic review of 37 papers concludes bluntly that there is an **absence of empirical evidence from real-world implementation**, and that small exporters and distributional effects are overlooked [7 in the MRV search / Wirdyansyah 2025].

All of it was calibrated before 1 January 2026, and **all of it predates the October 2025 Omnibus amendment**. The de minimis threshold alone reportedly removes obligations for around 90% of importers while retaining coverage of about 99% of embedded emissions — which redistributes exposure sharply away from small-consignment exporters and concentrates it on large ones. Nobody has recomputed African exposure under the rules that are actually in force.

**One sentence for your concept note:** *The exposure figures African trade negotiators are currently citing were computed under regulatory parameters that no longer apply, using a method that assumes the response rather than observing it.*

## 4. The research gap, stated precisely

Three gaps, in descending order of strength:

1. **No post-Omnibus, product-level exposure estimate for African exporters exists.** The rules changed in October 2025; the evidence base did not.
2. **The default-value penalty has never been quantified.** No study estimates, for real African installations, the wedge between actual emissions intensity and the default value assigned in its absence — the number that determines whether MRV investment pays for itself.
3. **There is no ex-post evidence of exporter response**, because until 1 January 2026 there was nothing to respond to. The literature's own systematic review says so.

Each of these survives the deletion test from the problem-statement template: remove the country name and the gap still reads as a gap. That is what you want.

## 5. Draft problem statement (~210 words — trim to fit your 800)

> The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026, and the first annual declarations and certificate surrenders fall due on 30 September 2027. African exposure is small in aggregate and severe in concentration: Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Cameroon rank among the most exposed economies in the world in relative terms [1], with aluminium, iron and steel, and fertiliser accounting for the bulk of it. The quantitative evidence base on which African negotiators rely is uniformly ex-ante simulation — multi-regional input-output analysis [1], [6], input-output inequality assessment [2], and computable general equilibrium modelling [3], [9], [10] — and a 2025 systematic review of the field concludes that empirical evidence from real-world implementation is absent altogether. That evidence base has now been overtaken twice: by the start of the definitive regime, and by the October 2025 Omnibus amendment, whose de minimis threshold removes obligations for roughly 90% of importers and redistributes exposure across exporter size classes. Separately, CBAM charges exporters who cannot document their emissions a conservative default value, a design that functions as a sanction on missing data and falls hardest on jurisdictions with the weakest verification infrastructure [4]. The share of Africa's CBAM liability attributable to *unverified* rather than *high* emissions has never been estimated, though it determines whether the appropriate response is decarbonisation or measurement.

## 6. Why this needs funding

Two arguments. Reviewers need both, and applicants usually give only the first.

### 6a. Why the research needs doing

- **The decision is live and dated.** African trade ministries and the AfCFTA Secretariat are formulating positions now, ahead of the first surrender deadline in September 2027 and ahead of EU scope-extension review. Evidence delivered in 2028 is commentary; evidence delivered in 2027 is an input.
- **The counterfactual is that the gap stays open.** No African institution is currently funded to produce this. The EU produces guidance for importers, not exposure analysis for exporters. Consultancies produce firm-level compliance advice, which is confidential by construction and never aggregates to a public number. Academic modellers produce simulations, which is precisely the mode that has been exhausted.
- **The remedy identified is cheap and actionable.** If the default-value wedge is large, the recommendation is a national MRV and verification facility — plausibly a low-single-digit-million-dollar intervention protecting export revenue orders of magnitude larger. Research that changes the ratio of a policy that size is a good use of $15,000.
- **It generates a reusable public good.** A published, product-level African exposure dataset under current rules, which negotiators, journalists and other researchers can use, and which can be updated as the phase-in factor rises.

### 6b. Why it cannot be done without money

Be specific here — this is where the budget justification lives, and vagueness reads as padding.

- **The novel component requires primary data.** Trade flows are free (Comtrade, ITC Trade Map). Emissions intensities of actual African installations are not. The actual-versus-default wedge — the whole contribution — can only be built from installation-level energy and process data obtained directly from exporters, under confidentiality, in person. Nobody sends that to a stranger over email.
- **Fieldwork is the cost.** Interviews with exporters, plant engineers, export promotion agencies and the national standards body require travel to industrial sites that are rarely near a capital city.
- **Emissions accounting needs a specialist.** Converting plant energy and process data into CBAM-methodology embedded emissions is engineering work, not economics. Budget for a part-time LCA or process-engineering consultant; doing it unassisted is how a paper gets refuted.
- **Time is the real input.** Twelve months of a researcher's attention has an opportunity cost, and pretending otherwise produces proposals that quietly assume free labour and then fail to deliver.
- **Dissemination is not free.** A validation workshop with the trade ministry and exporter association is what converts a paper into a policy input. It is also what the mentorship and publication side of YLR is designed to amplify.

### 6c. Budget that matches this method

| Line | US$ | Justification |

## 7. What $15,000 will not buy — say so, and say what you did instead

Do not promise a continental study. Promise **one country in depth plus a continental exposure layer built from secondary data**, and state the trade-off explicitly:

- Country case: full actual-versus-default wedge estimation, primary data, 15–20 interviews.
- Continental layer: post-Omnibus product-level exposure recomputation for all African exporters, from trade data and published intensities. No fieldwork, lower precision, but it is the first such estimate under current rules.

That structure gives a reviewer a deliverable they can picture and a scope they believe. Naming your own limitation is one of the strongest credibility signals available in an 800-word note.

## 8. Household-level extension, if you want to keep that thread

You asked previously for household-level work. CBAM is inherently a firm-and-trade instrument, but there is a legitimate household cut, and it is unoccupied:

**The company-town incidence of a CBAM shock.** Africa's exposed installations are single large employers anchoring identifiable local labour markets — a smelter, a fertiliser plant, the corridor of contractors and traders around them. If CBAM curtails output at one of these, the incidence lands on a geographically concentrated set of households whose earnings, enterprise revenue and consumption can be measured directly. Baseline the exposed labour market now, while the shock is still phasing in, using household survey data plus a targeted enterprise-and-worker survey in the plant's catchment. A pre-shock baseline in a place about to receive a dated, foreseeable shock is a genuinely valuable research asset, and it is only available before the phase-in bites.

This also lets you write a "just transition" framing that is about actual households rather than the abstraction the phrase usually denotes.

## 9. Before you cite any of this

1. **Read the consolidated Regulation** — (EU) 2023/956 as amended in October 2025 — for the phase-in factors, default-value methodology, and deadlines. Do not take these from blogs, news coverage, or this brief.
2. **Pull your country's actual trade data** at HS6 to the EU for CBAM-scope products, and check the numbers against the published exposure estimates. If they diverge, you have found your entry point.
3. **Check whether an exposure study for your country already exists** — several African governments commissioned consultancy work in 2024–25 that was never published. Ask the trade ministry before you propose to duplicate it.
4. **Secure one exporter contact before submitting.** A single named firm willing to talk turns the feasibility section from a hope into a plan.
