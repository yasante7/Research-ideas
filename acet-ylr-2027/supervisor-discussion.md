# Discussion with Kofi Amanor

A script for the conversation. Read it through once, then speak from the structure rather than the page. The bracketed notes are for you, not to be said aloud.

Aim for about twenty minutes of talking and leave the rest for his response. The single most important outcome is his agreement on the narrowing to fish smoking, because everything else follows from it.

---

## Part 1. Opening

*[Keep this short. Do not apologise for changing his idea. You developed it, which is what he asked for.]*

"Thank you for the note on green-smart technologies. I have taken it forward and I want to walk you through where it landed, because I made three decisions that move away from what you proposed, and I want to check them with you before I submit.

I am applying to the ACET Young Leaders in Research programme, 2027 cohort. It closes on the seventh of September. The grant is fifteen thousand dollars over twelve months, and the thematic area I am entering under is Green Industrial Transformation."

---

## Part 2. What I took from your email

*[Give him credit specifically. Name the parts you kept. This buys you the right to change the rest.]*

"Three things in your note shaped everything that followed.

The first was your warning about breadth. You said that if green-smart technology includes every environmentally beneficial household technology, the treatment becomes conceptually weak because solar lighting, clean cooking and solar irrigation work through different mechanisms. That was correct, and I narrowed much further than you suggested. I am now on one technology in one value chain.

The second was your distinction between labour-saving and productive-use technologies. I have kept it, but it now appears as a hypothesis to test rather than as a way of organising the sample.

The third was the question you raised near the end, and I think it is the best idea in the email. You asked why some households convert green technology into productive livelihood transitions while others use the same technology only for domestic consumption. That question is now the whole study. Everything else I have done is in service of answering it."

---

## Part 3. The first decision: the framing had to move from household to enterprise

"ACET has three thematic areas. Regional Integration, Innovation and Digital Policy, and Green Industrial Transformation. Green Industrial Transformation is the one that fits, and you were right that the green framing beats clean cooking alone for a climate funder.

But when I read how ACET describes that theme, it is about value-adding production, resilient supply chains, sustainable manufacturing, green jobs and green value chains. Every one of those is about firms and production. Your memo was framed at household level, on time use, unpaid domestic work and household income portfolios. To a reviewer sorting proposals against that theme, a household welfare study is a near miss however well it is designed.

So I kept your mechanisms and changed the unit of analysis. Released time became labour productivity in processing. Household income diversification became value addition and market participation. Post-harvest losses became value chain efficiency. Same study, same causal chain, but it now reads as industrial transformation rather than as welfare economics.

Your own recommendation is what made this possible. You suggested narrowing to women in food processing and trading. Agro-processing is value-adding production, so that suggestion was the bridge."

---

## Part 4. The second decision: the factorial trial is unaffordable

*[Be direct here. Do not soften it into vagueness. He proposed the design; you are showing him a budget constraint he did not have.]*

"You proposed a five-arm factorial experiment: control, labour-saving, productive-use, combined bundle, and combined-plus with enterprise support. I agree that is the right study. It is not a study this grant can fund.

The arms distribute capital equipment. A single solar cold-storage unit costs thousands of dollars. Powering five arms would put the study north of three hundred thousand. The grant is fifteen thousand. There is also a timing problem. The chain you described, from adoption through sustained use and productive use to livelihood transition and structural change, takes two to three years. The grant runs twelve months, which realistically gives me one round of data collection in months four to six.

So I dropped equipment distribution entirely and changed what has to be identified.

Here is the move. A standard comparison of adopters against non-adopters has to defeat selection into adoption, which is the hardest problem in this literature and is unwinnable on a single cross-section with no baseline. But if I sample only adopters and compare those who converted to productive use against those who did not, adoption is held constant and that problem disappears.

That does not make conversion exogenous, and I do not claim it does. The proposal claims a measurement and diagnostic contribution, not an impact estimate. I think that is defensible, and it is honest. What it gains is that the expensive part of the design was never necessary for the interesting question, which is the one you asked."

---

## Part 5. The third decision: what the literature forced me to change

*[This is where you demonstrate you did real work. Take your time.]*

"I ran a full literature review, and it changed the proposal in ways I did not expect. Four things stand out.

First, and this is uncomfortable, the thesis that green technology is not enough is not a novel finding. It is the settled prior. Pueyo and colleagues found Kenyan solar mini-grids produced no business gains after two years. Pelz and colleagues found that in electrified Indian villages, seventy-five per cent of connected enterprises draw under one kilowatt-hour a day, which is lighting and fans. A review of fifty-four studies concludes that energy inputs must be bundled with financial, human and institutional ones. If I had submitted that as the discovery, a reviewer would have known I had not read the field. So the contribution is now the magnitude of the gap and the ranking of what closes it, not its existence.

Second, there is a Ghanaian study that gives me a testable prediction instead of an open question. Fafchamps, McKenzie, Quinn and Woodruff randomised cash and in-kind grants to urban microenterprises here. Women running subsistence enterprises gained nothing from either. Larger women-owned firms grew, but only from in-kind grants, which they call the flypaper effect. Green equipment is in-kind capital, so that applies directly. It predicts conversion is non-linear in enterprise scale, near zero below a threshold and positive above it. I can test that.

Third, and this nearly sank the design. A study by Hing and colleagues compared sensors against surveys for cookstove use in India. Between twenty-eight and forty-six per cent of households over-reported, and the surveys missed long-term abandonment entirely, reporting adoption as unchanged while most households had stopped using stoves they had bought themselves. My headline measure is a conversion rate. If I measure it by asking people, I inherit that bias. Sensors are out of budget, so enumerators will inspect and photograph the equipment for function and evidence of recent use, and I will report the gap between what people say and what is verified. That gap becomes a second result.

Fourth, the outcome I had chosen was mismeasured. De Mel, McKenzie and Woodruff validated enterprise survey instruments against observers who visited firms fifteen or sixteen times a month. Firms underreport revenues by about thirty per cent, and asking profit directly is more accurate than detailed revenue-minus-expense questions. My original outcome, value added per labour hour, required exactly the noisier instrument. I now elicit profit directly and rebuild value added from physical quantities as a check."

---

## Part 6. Specific cases I want to look at

*[These make the study concrete. If he engages with anything, it will be these. Have them ready and be willing to defend each one.]*

"Let me be concrete about what I actually want to observe in the field. I have six cases in mind, and each one tests something specific.

**Case one. The processor who owns an Ahotor oven and still smokes on the Chorkor.** This is technology stacking, and it is well documented in the cookstove literature. I want to know what share of batches go through each oven, and what determines the split. My guess is that large orders and certain fish species go to the old oven because the new one is too slow or too small. If that is right, then partial conversion is the norm and a binary adoption measure is meaningless.

**Case two. Two adopters with the same oven, one certified and one not.** Ghanaian fisheries policy promotes higher-value markets through hygiene certification, and the Ahotor oven matters because it cuts polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Traditional ovens run at seven to ten times the European Union limit. So the oven is what makes certification possible. I want to find pairs who adopted at the same time, where one pursued certification and reached a formal buyer and the other still sells at the same landing site to the same traders. The difference between them is the study.

**Case three. The adopter whose equipment is broken.** In Ethiopia, Mekonnen and colleagues found that among people who abandoned improved stoves, over eighty per cent stopped because of breakage rather than preference. I want to record how long the equipment has been down, whether repair was attempted, what it would cost, and how far the nearest technician is. If breakage dominates, then after-sales service is the binding constraint and the policy answer is a service contract rather than more units.

**Case four. The woman with an oven whose husband runs a separate business.** Field and colleagues re-analysed data from Ghana, India and Sri Lanka and found that women's returns to capital were lower only in households where another member also ran an enterprise, because resources were redirected to the husband's business. It was not an ability gap. I can capture this with one roster question, and I want to compare those households against single-enterprise ones.

**Case five. The subsistence processor against the small commercial one.** One woman with a single oven, family labour, selling locally. Another with several ovens, hired workers, selling to traders who move fish inland or across the border. Fafchamps predicts conversion is near zero for the first and positive for the second. This is the scale threshold, and it is the case that decides whether targeting or bundling is the right policy.

**Case six. The cooperative dryer against the individually owned one.** In cassava and shea, solar dryers are often group assets. I want to compare group-owned equipment against individually owned, because the incentive to maintain and use a shared asset is different, and this is where I expect the second chain to earn its place in the study.

For sites, I am looking at landing beaches in the Central and Western regions for the fish component, Elmina, Apam, Moree and Shama being the obvious candidates, and cassava and shea processing in the Eastern and Northern regions for contrast. I have not confirmed programme presence at any of these yet, and that is the first thing I need to do."

---

## Part 7. What I need from you

*[Ask clearly. Do not bury the requests.]*

"Four things.

First, the sampling frame is my biggest risk. I have no existing data, so I have to build the frame. The best sources are pay-as-you-go solar providers, because their client records carry installation dates, which the event study needs. PEG and ZOLA Electric operate here. Beyond them there is the Association of Ghana Solar Industries, Apex Bank through the GEDAP appliance financing, and the SNV and USAID programmes that disseminated Ahotor ovens. If you know anyone at any of these, an introduction would be worth more to this proposal than anything else you could give me.

Second, I need a judgement from you on whether to keep the second value chain. Certification gives fish smoking a mechanism that cassava and shea cannot match, and a fish-only study is cheaper and tighter. The only reason to keep the second chain is the variation in enterprise scale that hypothesis one needs. If you think the fish sample alone spans enough scale, I will drop the others.

Third, would you be willing to act as one of my two referees? The application needs two, and they are the longest lead item.

Fourth, I would value your read on whether the diagnostic framing is too modest. I am not claiming causal impact, and I want to know if you think that reads as rigour or as timidity to this kind of panel."

## Things to hold your ground on

*[He may push back. These are the positions to defend.]*

- **The five-arm trial.** If he presses, the answer is the budget, and it is not negotiable. Offer to write the factorial design as the follow-on study the ACET grant would justify.
- **Not claiming causality.** A well-executed diagnostic beats a badly identified impact claim in front of a panel that knows the literature. Do not let this be talked back into an impact evaluation you cannot deliver.
- **The enterprise framing.** This is what makes the proposal eligible for the theme. If it drifts back to household welfare, it competes in a category ACET is not funding here.

## Things to concede readily

- The choice of second chain, or dropping it.
- Which regions and landing sites.
- The title.
- The order and weighting of the secondary outcomes.
