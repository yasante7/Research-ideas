# PURE × enterprise upgrading: which gaps survive verification
### Assessment of the uploaded Consensus gap report, and the fundable questions that come out of it

---

## 0. The headline problem with the report

The uploaded report maps gaps at the intersection of **Productive Use of Renewable Energy (PURE)** and **enterprise upgrading**, from a base of 13 references. Its central claim is that no study links PURE adoption at the micro-enterprise level to firm-level upgrading outcomes.

Before using any of it: **the report's gap map was drawn without the economics literature in it.**

Its citation base is systematic-review and management-journal work (*Cleaner Engineering and Technology*, *Renewable & Sustainable Energy Reviews*, *Technological Forecasting*), plus China-focused listed-firm studies and two WRI reports. It does not contain a single one of the papers an economist reviewing your application would consider foundational on this exact question:

| Missing from the report | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| Lee, Miguel & Wolfram, 2020, *Journal of Political Economy* [1] | RCT randomising grid connections in rural Kenya. **No meaningful medium-run economic impacts** |
| Dinkelman, 2011, *American Economic Review* [2] | South Africa's mass rural roll-out raised female employment; mechanism runs through microenterprise |
| Abeberese, 2017, *Review of Economics and Statistics* [3] | Higher electricity prices push Indian firms into **less electricity-intensive product mixes, lower machine intensity, lower productivity growth** |
| Abeberese, Ackah & Asuming, 2019, *World Bank Economic Review* [4] | Ghana rationing: outages cut productivity; firms cope by **shifting product mix**; generators don't insulate them |
| Kassem, 2024, *Journal of Development Economics* [5] | Indonesian grid expansion raises firm entry *and* exit, reallocating toward more productive firms |
| Meeks et al., 2024, *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management* [6] | Nepal microhydro mini-grids raised manufacturing establishments; **larger effects where market access is better** |

This matters practically. ACET funds economists, and its reviewers will know these papers. If you write "no study links energy to enterprise upgrading," Abeberese alone refutes you — her central finding is precisely that electricity constraints determine which products firms make and how fast their productivity grows. That is the energy→upgrading link, established in a top-five-adjacent journal, eight years ago.

**Use the report as a map of a management/energy-studies literature. Do not use its gap statements as written.**

## 1. Verification: which claimed gaps survive

| # | Claimed gap | Verdict | What decides it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | No study links PURE to firm-level upgrading | **Narrowed, still real** | The link exists for *formal manufacturing* via grid electricity [3], [4], [5]. It does **not** exist for *micro and small enterprises* using *decentralised renewables* in Africa, measured with upgrading metrics. Re-scope, don't abandon |
| 2 | Complementary inputs untested | **Survives — strongest** | Every study recommends bundling; none tests which complement binds [7], [8], [6]. The one documented bundling attempt failed [9] |
| 3 | Null/negative effects unexamined | **Reframe — becomes a better question** | Nulls are well established [1], [7], [10]. But cohort and case studies report large positives [11], [12]. The *contradiction* is unexplained. That is the paper |
| 4 | Outcome measurement gap | **Survives** | PURE work measures income, profits, opening hours [7], [10]. Upgrading work measures complexity and value addition. Genuinely unjoined at MSE scale |
| 5 | Gender underexamined | **Partly survives** | More than the one study the report found [2], [13], [11] — but women-led *enterprise* productive use remains thin. Best used as powered heterogeneity, not as the headline |
| 6 | Geographic concentration (China) | **Weak as stated** | "First in Africa" is a coverage claim, not a gap. Sharpen or drop (see §3) |
| 7 | Temporal shortfalls | **Real but unfixable by this grant** | You cannot close a longitudinal gap in 12 months. See §4 for the workaround |
| 8 | Definitional ambiguity | **Not fundable here** | ACET funds policy-relevant empirics, not concept papers |
| 9 | Post-COVID / geopolitical shifts | **Drop** | Too vague to identify |

## 2. The three questions worth pitching

### A. Which complement actually binds?
**ACET theme:** Green Industrial Transformation — near-verbatim fit with *"aligning finance, technology, and skills."*

**Problem statement (draft, ~230 words)**

> Africa's electrification strategy increasingly rests on decentralised renewables justified by their promise of productive use: mini-grids and solar systems are financed on the expectation that connected micro-enterprises will raise output, employment and value added. The rigorous evidence does not support that expectation on its own. A randomised expansion of grid connections in rural Kenya found no meaningful medium-run economic impacts [1]; a difference-in-differences evaluation of Kenyan solar mini-grids found that nearly two years after installation, business performance had not improved, with electricity consumption remaining low [7]; and staggered difference-in-differences across Ethiopia and Nigeria found electrification alone insufficient to shift non-farm entrepreneurship in the two-to-four years after connection [10].
>
> The literature converges on a single explanation — energy is necessary but not sufficient, and must be bundled with finance, equipment, skills and market access [7], [8]. Suggestive evidence supports this: mini-grid effects on manufacturing in Nepal were larger where market access was better [6], and appliance-financing raised mini-grid consumption by up to 66%, though the increase was not sustained and most borrowers did not repay in full [14]. The one documented attempt to implement a full bundle — procuring processing equipment and supporting entrepreneurs around a Tanzanian mini-grid — failed on regulatory risk, tariffs and management complexity [9].
>
> So the entire field prescribes bundling, one attempt at it failed, and **no study has established which complement is binding.** Programmes are being designed on an untested premise.

**Why this is fundable:** it is the design question a ministry of energy, a rural electrification agency, and every mini-grid developer needs answered before the next programme, and the answer changes budget allocation directly.

**Questions.** (i) Among PURE-adopting micro-enterprises, which constraint — appliance capital, working capital, technical skill, or market access — predicts whether electricity translates into output and value added? (ii) Do these constraints substitute or complement? (iii) What is the implied cost per unit of value added of each bundle configuration?

**Identification.** The honest answer for $15,000 is that you cannot run a four-arm RCT. What you can do: (a) exploit *existing programme heterogeneity* — many mini-grid sites received different complement packages for administrative reasons, giving quasi-experimental variation; (b) a constraint-elicitation design across a sample of connected enterprises, combining self-reported binding constraints with an incentivised choice task over hypothetical bundles; (c) matched comparison of enterprises in sites that received appliance financing versus those that did not. State the limitation plainly and propose the RCT as the follow-on the YLR grant positions you to win.

**Data.** Developer customer registries and consumption meter data (the highest-value ask — mini-grid operators hold per-connection consumption at high frequency and will often share it under NDA); enterprise survey n≈250 across 6–8 sites; programme documentation for complement assignment.

### B. Why do rigorous designs find nothing and case studies find transformation?
**ACET theme:** Green Industrial Transformation

**The gap.** This is the strongest *unforced* observation available, and the report missed it entirely because it lacked the economics papers.

Set the findings side by side. Randomised grid expansion in Kenya: no meaningful medium-run impacts [1]. Mini-grid DiD in Kenya: no business improvement after two years [7]. Staggered DiD in Ethiopia and Nigeria: insufficient alone [10]. Now the other column. A 2,658-respondent before-and-after cohort study of mini-grid connections in Kenya and Nigeria: **median income of rural Kenyan community members quadrupled** [11]. A Kenyan community micro-grid case study: **productivity per worker up 100–200%, incomes up 20–70%** [12]. Nepal microhydro: small but significant growth in manufacturing establishments [6].

These cannot all be right about the same phenomenon. The candidate explanations are distinguishable and testable: selection into connection; outcome definition (income versus profit versus value added); time horizon; sample attrition and survivorship in cohort designs; and genuine heterogeneity in market access and complement provision.

**Why this is fundable.** Governments are allocating capital on the strength of the optimistic column. A study that establishes *which* column to believe, and under what conditions each is right, is worth more to a finance ministry than another point estimate. Reconciliation papers are also disproportionately citable.

**Method.** Systematic re-analysis: assemble the studies, harmonise outcome definitions, and test whether design type predicts effect size after conditioning on outcome measure and horizon — a small meta-analysis with design as the moderator. Then a primary validation: in one setting, measure the *same* enterprises with both the cohort instrument and the rigorous comparison design, and show directly how much of the gap is method. **That second component is the contribution**, and it is exactly the size of study $15,000 buys.

### C. Does energy scarcity push African micro-enterprises down the product ladder?
**ACET theme:** Green Industrial Transformation, with direct purchase on ACET's *Growth with DEPTH* — Productivity and Technology upgrading

**The gap, sharply.** Abeberese established for Indian manufacturing that when electricity gets more expensive, firms **switch to less electricity-intensive production processes within narrowly defined industries, reduce machine intensity, and grow productivity more slowly** — so electricity constraints limit growth by pushing firms into industries with fewer productivity-enhancing opportunities [3]. The Ghana study replicates the coping-by-product-mix mechanism under rationing [4].

Both study **formal, registered manufacturing firms in census or survey frames.** The segment that dominates African employment — informal micro and small enterprises — has never been tested for this mechanism, despite being the segment PURE programmes actually reach, and despite having far fewer margins to adjust on.

This is a *theory transplant* question, which is a strong shape: an established mechanism, validated where firms are formal and capital markets function, tested where neither holds.

**Questions.** (i) Do African MSEs facing higher or less reliable energy costs operate a systematically less energy-intensive, lower-complexity product mix? (ii) Does PURE adoption move the product mix *up*, or only reduce the cost of the existing mix? (iii) Is the adjustment margin product mix, hours, or exit?

**Why it matters for policy.** If PURE only lowers the cost of what enterprises already make, it is a welfare transfer, not an industrial policy — and should be financed and evaluated as such. If it shifts the product mix upward, it belongs in the industrial strategy. Ministries currently claim the second and fund the first.

**Data.** Enterprise modules in LSMS-type panels for the product-mix baseline; a primary survey measuring product lines, equipment, and energy intensity per line; spatial variation in outage exposure or tariff; developer consumption data where obtainable.

## 3. Two framings to avoid

**"First study in Africa."** Delete your country name and the gap disappears — that is the test, and geographic novelty fails it. If you want the regional angle, the defensible version is a *measurement* claim: the upgrading literature's metrics (patent counts, product complexity indices, listed-firm TFP) presuppose a formal firm panel that most African economies do not have, so the question of whether upgrading is even measurable in an informal MSE economy is itself open. That is a real gap. "Nobody did it here" is not.

**"PURE has a definitional problem."** True, and unfundable. ACET wants a number a minister can act on.

---

## 4. What $15,000 and 12 months cannot do here — say it before a reviewer does

The report's temporal gap is real: PURE evaluations capture two-year windows while upgrading unfolds over much longer horizons. **You cannot close that gap with this grant, and claiming you will is the fastest way to lose credibility.**

Two legitimate workarounds, either of which turns the constraint into a design feature:

1. **Borrow the time.** Use existing multi-wave panels (LSMS-ISA runs 2010–2023 in several countries) to get the long horizon from secondary data, and spend the grant on the primary measurement the panels lack.
2. **Retrospective cohort with objective anchors.** Sample enterprises that adopted PURE five or more years ago and reconstruct trajectories using verifiable anchors — equipment purchase records, connection dates from developer registries, meter histories — rather than recall alone. Developer registries make this feasible in a way it would not be for household recall.

Say which one you are doing and why, in the note. Naming a limitation you cannot solve, and showing the workaround, reads as competence. Ignoring it reads as inexperience.

---

## 5. Recommendation

**Pitch A (which complement binds).** It has the cleanest policy hook — it changes how programme money is split — it matches ACET's theme language almost word for word, it uses the null results as motivation rather than fighting them, and it is executable at this budget if you secure developer data. It also sets up an RCT follow-on, which is a good thing to be able to say in the leadership section.

**B is the higher-ceiling paper** and the more original observation, but it is a methods paper wearing a policy jacket, and ACET's reviewers are policy people. Pitch it only if you can make the reconciliation land as "which evidence should the ministry believe."

**C is the most intellectually elegant** and the easiest to explain to an economist, but hardest to execute — measuring product mix and energy intensity per product line in informal enterprises is genuinely difficult fieldwork.

**Versus the carbon-finance note already drafted:** that one has the sharper single hook (a quantified sovereign liability under Article 6) and a deliverable an agency can adopt as a procedure. This PURE cluster has broader ACET-theme fit and a more conventional development-economics framing. If you want one submission, the carbon note is more distinctive. If the PURE topic is closer to your existing work and contacts — which matters more than either — take A, and lead with the fact that everyone prescribes bundling and nobody has tested it.

**The decisive question is not which idea is better in the abstract. It is which one you can get data for.** For A, that means one mini-grid developer willing to share customer registries and meter data. Email them this week; the answer determines the pitch.
