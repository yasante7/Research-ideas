The strategic call

Framing

Kofi Amanor's memo closed on a conditional: the green-smart framing is stronger for a climate-development funder, while clean-cooking-only stays more competitive for health, gender, or energy-access grants. ACET's three themes are Regional Integration, Innovation and Digital Policy, and Green Industrial Transformation. The conditional resolves cleanly — the green framing wins, and clean-cooking-only would be the weaker submission.

But there is a second problem the memo does not address. ACET describes this theme as climate-smart, circular, value-adding production and resilient supply chains, with sub-areas covering green industrial policy, sustainable manufacturing, green jobs and green value chains. Every one of those is about production, firms and value chains. The memo is framed almost entirely at the level of household livelihoods — time use, unpaid domestic work, household income portfolios. To a reviewer sorting proposals against this theme, that is a thematic near-miss however well the study is designed.

The fix is already in the memo: its recommended narrowing to women in food processing and trading. Agro-processing is value-adding production. But it only works if the unit of analysis and the vocabulary move with it.

Same study, same mechanisms, same data. But do green technologies move informal food processors from subsistence into market-oriented value-adding production? is a Green Industrial Transformation question. Does solar reduce women's unpaid domestic hours? is not — for this funder.

What the study measures

Thesis

Ghana's Renewable Energy Master Plan, donor fisheries programmes and commercial solar distributors all report the same kind of number: units installed, ovens disseminated, cold storage financed. That number is a procurement outcome. Industrial transformation happens three steps later.

Adoption is a procurement outcome. Industrial transformation requires that the technology raise productivity, add value and move output into wider markets. Policy cannot price what it cannot see.

Why the factorial RCT had to go

Design

The memo proposed a five-arm factorial trial distributing solar refrigeration, dryers and processing equipment: control, labour-saving, productive-use, combined bundle, and combined-plus. It is the right study. It is not a study this call can fund.

The pivot that rescues it

The sharpest question in the memo — why do some adopters convert to productive use while others do not? — turns out to be both the most original and the most affordable, because it changes what has to be identified.

A conventional adopters-versus-non-adopters comparison has to defeat selection into adoption. On a cross-section with no baseline and no existing data, that is unwinnable. Sampling only adopters, and comparing those who converted to productive use against those who did not, holds adoption constant and sidesteps the problem entirely.

This does not make conversion exogenous — the same unobservables plausibly drive both. So the note claims a measurement and diagnostic contribution rather than an impact estimate. That is honest, defensible, and still novel: no dataset currently reports a conversion rate.

The discrete choice experiment is what makes this a research design rather than a survey. It is genuinely experimental, it costs nothing beyond instrument development, and it yields a quotable policy result: the ranked marginal willingness to expand production per complementary input — finance, technical skills, market linkage, after-sales service.

What the evidence changed

Literature · pass 1

The search moved the proposal in four ways. The largest is uncomfortable: the memo's central thesis is not a finding — it is the settled prior.

1. "Technology is not enough" is already established

Solar mini-grids in Kenya produced no improvement in business performance two years on, with consumption flat and local demand unmoved (Pueyo et al. 2018). In electrified North Indian villages, 34% of micro-enterprises had no connection at all and 75% of those connected drew under 1 kWh a day — lighting and fans (Pelz et al. 2021). A review of 54 studies concludes energy inputs must be bundled with financial, human-capability and institutional ones (Aarakit et al. 2024).

Claiming green technology is not enough as the discovery would read as not knowing the field. The contribution has to be the magnitude of the gap and the ranking of what closes it.

2. The capital literature gives you a prediction — and its canonical study is Ghanaian

Green equipment is in-kind capital, so what is known about capital drops into microenterprises applies directly. In urban Ghana, women running subsistence enterprises gained nothing from cash or in-kind grants, while larger women-owned firms grew from in-kind capital only — the flypaper effect, where capital arriving as goods stays in the business (Fafchamps et al. 2013).

That implies conversion is non-linear in baseline enterprise scale — near zero below a threshold, positive above it. A falsifiable prediction turns a descriptive survey into a theory-testing study.

And a sharper gender mechanism than "norms": women's returns were lower only in households where another member also ran a business, because capital was redirected there — not an ability gap (Field et al. 2019). One roster question measures it.

3. Self-reported use would have sunk the headline result

This was the most dangerous finding. Sensors compared against surveys for cookstoves in rural India found 28–46% of households over-reporting, and surveys missed long-term dis-adoption entirely — reporting adoption as unchanged while most households had stopped using stoves they had bought with their own money. Some with zero recorded use still reported fuel savings and less smoke (Hing et al. 2023).

A study whose headline is a conversion rate measured by asking people walks straight into that bias. Sensors are out of budget; enumerator verification is not — equipment inspected for function and evidence of recent use, photographed, breakdown history dated. Report both measures and the gap between them, and the gap becomes a second publishable result extending that finding to productive assets in Africa.

The gap that survived

Ghana-specific evidence is thin and dated — solar PV and rural micro-enterprises studied in 2010, and through the lens of trading hours after sunset (Obeng et al. 2010). The Ahotor oven has been studied for adoption drivers and for PAH levels, but never for enterprise outcomes — and since traditional ovens exceed EU limits by seven to ten times, oven type plausibly gates access to formal and export buyers. That is a value-chain argument, which is exactly what the theme wants.

The closest competitor is worth naming in the note rather than avoiding: 700 Nigerian MSMEs, propensity-score matched, solar adoption associated with 23–27% higher monthly earnings (Owusu-Sekyere et al. 2024). Same method family, adjacent country, two years old. They estimate adoption to earnings across general MSMEs; you measure adoption to conversion to value addition inside one chain, and rank what closes it.

And a policy hook worth taking

Results-based financing in off-grid solar pays suppliers per verified device deployed. If a large share of deployed productive-use equipment never enters production, that instrument is priced against the wrong metric — a concrete, fundable argument with a named policy instrument attached, rather than a call for "complementary support".

What the deep search corrected

Literature · pass 2

The first pass fixed the framing. The second pass found three errors in the design itself — each one a thing a reviewer with development-economics training would have caught.

1. The primary outcome was mismeasured

de Mel, McKenzie & Woodruff validated enterprise survey instruments against observers who visited firms 15–16 times a month. Firms underreport revenues by roughly 30%; account diaries move reported revenues and expenses but not profits; and asking profit directly is more accurate than detailed revenue-minus-expense elicitation (2009, JDE).

"Value added per labour hour" required exactly the noisier instrument. Profit is now elicited directly; value added is rebuilt from physical quantities — kilogrammes processed, fuel used, price received — as a secondary check, since processors recall quantities better than money.

2. Energy in levels would have been read backwards

Firm-level rebound combines factor substitution with output expansion, and fuel rebound runs 24–80% in energy-intensive sectors (review). A field experiment giving energy audits and engineer support to Indian manufacturers found firms afterwards used more energy — energy complements other inputs, so the factor mix shifted (World Bank review). And the Duke evaluation of Kenyan solar-pump adopters found hired labour rose — production expanding, not just time reallocating (2023–25).

If conversion succeeds, output expands and total fuelwood spend may rise. The first draft would have scored that as failure. Energy is now measured as cost per kilogramme processed.

3. The qualitative work was sequenced backwards

Methodological guidance for choice experiments in low-income settings is explicit: secondary literature can suggest attributes, but primary formative research is almost always needed to validate them before fielding (Health Policy and Planning). All 40 interviews sat after the survey. Now split — 15 formative in month 3 to validate and price the attributes, 25 explanatory in month 6.

Adoption Is Not Transformation: Green Technology and Value Addition in Ghana's Food Processing Micro-Enterprises

The policy problem

Green industrial policy in Africa is financed against units deployed. Results-based financing pays suppliers per verified device installed; national plans report dryers, ovens and cold storage disseminated. Deployment is a procurement outcome. Green industrialisation — low-carbon production, value addition, decent jobs — requires that equipment be used to make something more valuable. If much deployed equipment never enters production, these instruments are priced against the wrong metric, and no one knows the share.

Positioning

This is an energy-efficiency gap in informal firms. Allcott and Greenstone (2012) model why firms leave privately profitable energy investments unexploited; audited European SMEs adopt only 40–48% of recommended measures. The same gap appears in low-income enterprise settings: Kenyan solar mini-grids produced no business gains after two years (Pueyo et al. 2018), and 75% of connected enterprises in electrified Indian villages draw under 1 kWh daily (Pelz et al. 2021). Yet where identification is strong the mechanism works — South African electrification raised female employment 13.5 points by releasing women from home production into microenterprise (Dinkelman 2011). Separately, urban Ghanaian evidence shows capital alone fails: women's subsistence enterprises gained nothing from cash or in-kind grants, while larger women-owned firms grew only from in-kind capital (Fafchamps et al. 2013). No study joins these when the capital is the green technology.

Research question

Conditional on adoption, what converts green processing technology into value addition — and which complementary input binds?

Two hypotheses, not open questions. Fafchamps et al. implies conversion is non-linear in baseline enterprise scale: near zero below a threshold, positive above. Field et al. (2019) implies conversion fails where the household contains a second enterprise, because returns are redirected there.

Setting

Coastal fish smoking, where women are 96.9% of processors. Traditional Chorkor ovens emit polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at seven to ten times EU limits; fuel-efficient Ahotor ovens cut them. Because Ghanaian fisheries policy promotes higher-value markets through hygiene certification, the conversion pathway is concrete and measurable: oven → lower PAH → certification eligibility → formal and export buyers. Informal cross-border trade in processed pelagics alone runs to roughly 6,000 tonnes and USD 14 million annually. The Ahotor literature covers adoption drivers and PAH but never enterprise outcomes. Inland cassava and shea processing, using solar drying and powered milling, supplies contrast and the enterprise-scale variation the first hypothesis needs.

500 enterprises — some 300 adopters, 200 non-adopters in the same clusters. Pay-as-you-go solar providers and Ahotor dissemination programmes hold client records carrying installation dates, which the event study requires; cluster enumeration at landing sites captures off-list adopters.

Identification and measurement

A retrospective enterprise panel supports an event study with enterprise fixed effects around adoption timing. A converter/non-converter comparison holds adoption constant, removing selection into adoption while remaining explicitly diagnostic. A discrete choice experiment — cleanly experimental — randomises support packages across capital form, training, supervision, after-sales service and certification linkage, each attribute drawn from a documented finding and validated in formative interviews before fielding. Three hundred adopters across eight tasks yield 2,400 observations for mixed logit.

Two measurement decisions follow the evidence. Profit is elicited directly, since detailed revenue-and-expense questions are less accurate and firms underreport revenues by roughly 30% (de Mel et al. 2009); value added is constructed from physical quantities as a secondary check. And use is verified by enumerator inspection — functionality, evidence of recent use, photographs — because survey self-report over-states stove use by 28–46% and misses dis-adoption entirely (Hing et al. 2023). The reported-versus-verified gap is itself a reported result.

Outcomes

Primary: enterprise profit per hour of processing labour. Secondary: verified productive use; certification status and buyer type; months operated; paid employment; spoilage; energy cost per kilogramme processed; women's control over revenue. Energy is measured as intensity, not level — if conversion succeeds, output expands and total fuel spend may rise. With roughly 165 converters against 135 non-converters, the design detects 0.33 standard deviations at 80% power.

Contribution and policy

The first verified conversion rate for green processing technology in Africa, and an experimental ranking of what closes the gap. The nearest studies estimate adoption-to-earnings associations — Nigerian MSMEs by matching (Owusu-Sekyere et al. 2024), Kenyan solar irrigation adopters against non-adopters (Duke/Shell Foundation 2025); neither measures conversion or identifies the binding constraint.

Green industrial support is meant to be conditional, performance-based and tied to value addition. Results-based financing currently conditions on deployment because conversion is unmeasured. This study makes it measurable.
