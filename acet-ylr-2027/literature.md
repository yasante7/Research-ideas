# Literature Review — The Enterprise Dimension

Two passes: an academic-database sweep (Consensus, 220m papers) and a deep working-paper, repository and grey-literature search. Organised by what each body of work does to the proposal.

**Six findings changed the design.** They are marked ⚑ and collected in §9.

---

## 1. "Technology is not enough" is the settled prior, not a finding

The productive-use-of-energy literature has repeatedly shown that energy access alone does not improve enterprise performance.

- **Pueyo et al. (2018), Kenya.** Difference-in-differences on solar mini-grids: nearly two years after installation, **no improvement in business performance**. Consumption stayed low; local demand did not pick up. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/432e4937764a589083b57cb9065e310b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Pelz et al. (2021), North India.** Of 2,004 micro- and small enterprises in near-fully electrified villages, **34% had no connection at all**, and **75% of the connected consumed under 1 kWh/day** — lighting and fans. Connection tracked wealth, not supply quality. The conversion gap, quantified. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6fdfdee6b0a358d7a2c39bc53f2596ad/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Neelsen & Peters (2011), Uganda.** 200 micro-enterprises: electrification changed the capital mix and sectoral distribution but produced **no expansionary effect on profits or wages**. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d87dd93008e85ef5a46616d2cb45d82f/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Aarakit et al. (2024)**, systematic review of 54 studies: electricity inputs must be "bundled with … financial, technological, human capabilities, institutional, infrastructural, social, demographic" factors. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8aafb1d57e395f7ea285cfa5cfd3e39d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**The counterweight — the mechanism does work when identified well.** Dinkelman (2011, *AER*) exploits South Africa's rural electrification roll-out with an IV/fixed-effects design and finds female employment rises **13.5 percentage points** within five years, with no male effect. The stated mechanism is precisely the one the originating memo proposed: **releasing women from home production and enabling microenterprises**. (It also finds female wages fall and male earnings rise — a general-equilibrium effect a micro study will not capture.) → [AER](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.101.7.3078) · [working paper PDF](https://conference.iza.org/TAM2009/dinkelman_t4981.pdf)

**Implication.** The memo's thesis is credible but not novel. The contribution must be **the magnitude of the gap and the ranking of what closes it**.

## 2. ⚑ A theoretical home: this is an energy-efficiency gap in informal firms

The strongest framing available, and absent from both the memo and the first draft. **Allcott & Greenstone (2012, *JEP*), "Is There an Energy Efficiency Gap?"** models investment in energy-using capital under two failures: uninternalised externalities, and frictions such as imperfect information that stop firms exploiting *privately profitable* efficiency investments. → [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.26.1.3) · [NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17766/w17766.pdf)

The empirical regularity is exactly the conversion gap in a formal setting: German SMEs subjected to energy audits adopted only about **40%** of recommended measures; a Swedish audit programme produced **48%**. → [firm survey evidence](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0140988324002068)

And a warning from the developing-country frontier: a field experiment offering **industrial energy audits plus engineer support** to energy-intensive Indian manufacturers found firms subsequently used **more** energy — because energy complements other inputs, so firms changed their labour and capital mix. → [World Bank review of green-technology impact evaluations](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/there-any-impact-evaluation-evidence-government-policies-green-technology)

**Why this matters.** Framing the study as an energy-efficiency gap among informal firms gives it a home in mainstream economics rather than in the energy-access literature alone, and supplies a ready theoretical vocabulary — information frictions, principal-agent problems, behavioural failures — for why conversion fails.

## 3. The microenterprise-capital literature makes sharp predictions, and its canonical study is Ghanaian

Green equipment *is* in-kind capital, so everything known about capital drops applies.

- **Fafchamps, McKenzie, Quinn & Woodruff (2013), Ghana.** Cash vs. in-kind grants to urban microenterprises. **Women running subsistence enterprises gained nothing from either.** For larger women-owned firms, only *in-kind* grants produced growth — the **flypaper effect**: capital arriving as goods stays in the business, cash does not. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8e73c1e5b65e5a8b9ef2b509754a8a9e/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **de Mel, McKenzie & Woodruff (2008), Sri Lanka.** Returns to capital indistinguishable from zero for women. Men spent grants on working capital, **women on equipment** — and the women saw no returns. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b225da0e78f85b3d9580a7523716e0a9/?utm_source=claude_desktop) · [5-year follow-up, *Science*](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3d1a4e457b52561b95d54730219a361e/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Field et al. (2019)**, re-analysis across Ghana, India and Sri Lanka. Women's returns were lower **only where another household member also ran a business** — capital was redirected, not misused. Not an ability gap. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7e4f32d7b23752dc93dcf83aa2103f11/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Berge, Bjorvatn & Tungodden (2015), Tanzania.** Grant × training factorial: strong effects for men, muted for women. "**Business training is essential to transform financial capital into productive investments.**" → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3fa1f260b5cc5c9c8f1713073bcec1ad/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Blattman et al. (2016), Uganda — the counterpoint.** $150 cash + 5 days training + **ongoing supervision** doubled enterprise ownership and incomes among ultrapoor women; varying supervision showed it **raised business survival**. Bundles can work for women. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/69edf59836de51a8aaa732e206240eb6/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Fiala (2018), Uganda.** Loans worked for men, grants did not; nothing for women. Repayment obligation appears to raise the odds of productive investment. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f06d62f18dd9587eb77381a284fc5441/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **de Mel et al. (2012), Sri Lanka.** Training alone: no profit impact. Training + grant: large gains that **dissipated in year two**. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/07b07667ce2b5e0bac3ecacf6c3f2753/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**⚑ (a) A falsifiable prediction.** Fafchamps et al. implies conversion is **non-linear in baseline enterprise scale** — near zero below a threshold, positive above it. Testable directly; it turns a descriptive study into a theory-testing one.

**⚑ (b) A cheap, sharp gender mechanism.** Field et al. predicts conversion failure concentrates in households containing a **second enterprise**. One roster question captures it.

## 4. ⚑ The primary outcome as drafted was mismeasured

**de Mel, McKenzie & Woodruff (2009, *JDE*), "Measuring microenterprise profits: don't ask how the sausage is made."** They validated survey instruments against research assistants observing firms 15–16 times in a month, randomly allocated account books, and asked firms how much firms like theirs underreport. Findings:

- Firms **underreport revenues by roughly 30%**
- Account diaries move reported revenues and expenses but **not profits**
- **Asking profit directly is more accurate than detailed revenue-minus-expense elicitation**

→ [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=985001) · [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/deveco/v88y2009i1p19-31.html)

**This contradicted the first draft.** "Value added per hour of processing labour" requires exactly the detailed revenue-and-input elicitation shown to be *noisier* than the simple question. Fix in §9.

## 5. ⚑ Self-reported use would not have survived review

- **Hing et al. (2023), rural India.** Sensors vs. surveys on cookstove use: **28–46% of households over-reported**; sensors showed most had dis-adopted stoves *they bought with their own money*; **surveys missed the decline entirely**, reporting adoption as unchanged. Some households with *zero* recorded use reported fuel savings and less smoke. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b73b4b92f0245cdabe4991c86c9c8fc4/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Ruiz-Mercado et al. (2011)**, 461 citations — the canonical adoption-vs-sustained-use framework, arguing for objective monitoring over self-report. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/903e73db122d5b19bc5b8756d2a570e0/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Mekonnen et al. (2020), Ethiopia.** 63% of Mirt stoves in place after 3.5 years; among abandoners, **over 80% stopped because of breakage**, not preference. Maintenance, not motivation. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4ba925b3a7095b188ce2a2edcc31680d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

A conversion rate measured by asking people walks into a documented 28–46% bias. Fix in §9.

## 6. ⚑ Energy outcomes must be intensities, not levels

The rebound literature and the Indian audit experiment point the same way. Firm-level rebound is the joint effect of **factor substitution and output expansion**; fuel rebound has been estimated at **24–80%** across energy-intensive sectors, and output effects dominate where product demand is elastic. → [review of economy-wide rebound evidence](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1364032121000769) · [industrial rebound decomposition](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12053-022-10059-4)

Corroborated on the productive-use side: the **Duke/Shell Foundation SunCulture evaluation** (2023–25, 750+ Kenyan smallholders, six counties) found solar pump adopters raised irrigation from 72% to 95%, doubled irrigated plot share from 23% to ~50%, and **increased use of hired labour** — "not only reallocating internal work but actively expanding production." → [Shell Foundation](https://shellfoundation.org/news/how-solar-irrigation-is-transforming-the-lives-of-smallholder-farmers-in-kenya-lessons-and-the-road-ahead/)

**Consequence.** If conversion succeeds, output expands and **total fuelwood spend may rise**. Reading that as failure would be exactly wrong. Energy outcomes must be specified per unit of output.

That evaluation is also the closest live precedent for the design — adopter vs. non-adopter comparison, productive-use solar, Africa, recent, well-funded. Differentiate: they study solar irrigation among farm households; this studies processing equipment among enterprises, measures a conversion rate, and ranks constraints.

## 7. ⚑ The Ghana setting has a concrete conversion mechanism — and named sampling frames

**Certification is the pathway to higher-value markets.** Ghanaian fisheries governance and development actors promote access to higher-value-added markets **through fish hygienic standard certification** for women processors and traders. Traditional Chorkor ovens produce PAH **7–10× above the EU standard**; the Ahotor oven's fat collector cuts PAH concentrations. So the causal chain is concrete and measurable:

> Ahotor oven → lower PAH → certification eligibility → higher-value and export buyers → value addition

This is far stronger than generic "market access," and it is a *value chain* argument, which is what the ACET theme wants. → [Market women's skills, constraints, and agency in Ghana](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40152-022-00279-w) · [Ahotor adoption drivers](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949824424000417) · [oven type and smoked fish quality](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389022157_The_Effects_of_Oven_Type_on_the_Quality_of_Smoked_Fish_Comparison_Between_the_Use_of_the_Chorkor_and_Ahotor_Ovens_in_Ghana) · [SFMP market development report](https://www.crc.uri.edu/download/GH2014_ACT242_RESONANCE_FIN508.pdf)

**Sector facts worth citing.** Women are **96.9%** of surveyed Ghanaian fish processors and traders. Informal cross-border trade in processed small pelagics runs to roughly **6,000 MT and US$14 million a year**. Documented constraints: fish supply, **credit access**, hygiene, storage, transport and market governance; lacking collateral, women rely on savings groups and informal loans. → [informal cross-border trade](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40152-022-00284-z) · [post-harvest associations and the fish market](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0308597X24005360) · [grassroots entrepreneurship of women fish processors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2452292925000323)

**Named frame targets — this was the biggest open risk.**

| Source | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **PAYGO providers — PEG, ZOLA Electric** | Digital client records carry **installation dates and payment histories**: exactly the adoption timing the event study needs |
| **Association of Ghana Solar Industries** | GEDAP partner; distributed 15,000+ solar home systems with Apex Bank |
| **Apex Bank / SME lenders** | Administered GEDAP appliance financing — 50% end-user, 50% bank-financed over three years |
| **GEDAP Volta Lake mini-grids** | Five pilot sites explicitly for productive uses including **fishing community livelihoods**, ~10,000 beneficiaries |
| **Ahotor programmes — SNV, USAID/SFMP** | Oven dissemination records by landing site |

→ [Ghana off-grid solar market assessment](https://rise.esmap.org/sites/default/files/library/ghana/Electricity%20Access/Ghana_Off%20grid%20solar%20Market%20Assessment.pdf) · [GOGLA Ghana country brief](https://gogla.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Ghana-Country-Brief.pdf) · [GEDAP](https://www.iea.org/policies/4954-ghana-energy-development-and-access-project-gedap) · [Renewable Energy Master Plan](https://energycom.gov.gh/rett/phocadownload/remp/Draft-Renewable-Energy-Masterplan.pdf)

**Existing Ghana evidence remains thin.** Obeng et al. (2010) studied solar PV and rural micro-enterprises but through trading hours after sunset, fifteen years ago → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b2172e61026b580c892f94bf2285942f/?utm_source=claude_desktop); Owusu et al. (2022) used n=50, convenience-sampled, Accra only → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c0770706af91582994e25b82cf0dd1d3/?utm_source=claude_desktop). The Ahotor oven has been studied for adoption drivers and PAH, never for enterprise outcomes.

## 8. ⚑ The choice experiment needs formative fieldwork before the survey, not after

Methodological guidance is explicit: although secondary literature can identify an initial attribute set, **in low-income settings additional primary research is almost always necessary** to ensure the final attributes are appropriate and valid. → [How to do (or not to do) … Designing a discrete choice experiment for application in a low-income country, *Health Policy and Planning*](https://academic.oup.com/heapol/article/24/2/151/591265)

Precedent for DCEs with entrepreneurs exists — 625 randomly selected Philippine entrepreneurs were surveyed on climate-resilience strategies — and for using DCEs to design development interventions. → [service design and DCEs in complex settings, *World Development*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305750X22001887)

**The first draft sequenced all 40 interviews after the survey.** That is backwards for attribute validation. Fix in §9.

**A prior worth testing.** A Delphi exercise with 150 stakeholders reached consensus that **access to finance** is the key barrier to cold-storage adoption in rural value chains, noting median smallholder income in Kenya of about **USD 28/month**. Framing the DCE as a test of that stakeholder consensus — does revealed ranking match what the sector believes? — is a sharper pitch than open-ended exploration. → [barriers to solar cold storage, Tanzania](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2022.990528/full)

**Complementarity precedent.** A 1,200-household Uganda RCT found improved maize storage made farmers **10pp more likely to plant hybrid maize** and cut storage losses — technology adoption enabling further upgrading, which is the conversion channel in another crop. → [paper](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6473560/)

## 9. What changes in the proposal

| ⚑ | Change | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Frame as an **energy-efficiency gap among informal firms**; drop novelty claims on "technology is not enough" | Allcott & Greenstone 2012; Pueyo 2018; Pelz 2021 |
| 2 | **Elicit profit directly**; build value added from physical quantities as a secondary construction, not the primary instrument | de Mel et al. 2009 |
| 3 | **Energy outcomes per unit of output**, never in levels — rising fuelwood spend alongside expansion is success | rebound evidence 24–80%; Indian audit experiment; SunCulture |
| 4 | **Split the qualitative work**: ~15 formative interviews before the survey to validate DCE attributes, ~25 explanatory after | *Health Policy and Planning* DCE guidance |
| 5 | Make **certification the named conversion pathway**, not generic market access | Ghana fisheries certification literature; PAH 7–10× EU limits |
| 6 | Name **PAYGO client records** as the primary frame — they carry adoption dates | GEDAP, GOGLA, market assessment |
| 7 | Add **enumerator-verified use**; report the reported-vs-verified gap as a result | Hing et al. 2023 |
| 8 | Add the **non-linearity in enterprise scale** and **second-enterprise** hypotheses | Fafchamps et al. 2013; Field et al. 2019 |
| 9 | Position policy against **conditional, performance-based** industrial support — re-specify RBF on conversion, not deployment | ACET green industrialization framing |
| 10 | Cite and differentiate from **Owusu-Sekyere et al. 2024** and the **Duke SunCulture evaluation** | scoop risk |

### Literature-derived DCE attributes

| Attribute | The finding it answers |
|---|---|
| Capital form — in-kind vs. cash | Flypaper effect (Fafchamps et al. 2013) |
| Technical and business training | Training transforms capital into productive investment (Berge et al. 2015) |
| Ongoing supervision / follow-up | Supervision raised business survival (Blattman et al. 2016) |
| After-sales and maintenance | 80%+ of abandonment was breakage (Mekonnen et al. 2020) |
| Market linkage / certification support | Demand does not pick up alone (Pueyo et al. 2018); certification gates higher-value buyers (Ghana fisheries) |

Attributes are literature-derived but **provisional until the formative interviews confirm them**, per the DCE guidance above.

### Alignment with ACET's own framing

ACET defines green industrialization as growing African economies through **low-carbon manufacturing and value addition** while delivering **decent jobs**, and the surrounding literature argues industrial support should be **conditional, performance-based and time-bound**, tied to technology transfer and value addition rather than protection. The proposal's core recommendation — re-specify results-based financing against a conversion metric rather than units deployed — sits squarely inside that doctrine. Worth mirroring the vocabulary. → [Toward Green Industrialization in Africa (ACET)](https://acetforafrica.org/research-and-analysis/insights-ideas/toward-green-industrialization-in-africa-a-new-chapter-for-climate-and-economic-transformation/) · [Green Industrial Policy and Industrialisation in Africa](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-83829-3_3)

### Scoop risk

**Owusu-Sekyere et al. (2024, *Energy Policy*)** — 700 Nigerian MSMEs, propensity-score matching, solar adoption associated with **23–27% higher monthly earnings** and 36.6% lower energy expenditure. Same method family, adjacent country. → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2db34509d6135883b6d066f6dd1c78dd/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Duke / Shell Foundation SunCulture evaluation (2023–25)** — adopter vs. non-adopter comparison, 750+ households, six Kenyan counties. → [link](https://shellfoundation.org/news/how-solar-irrigation-is-transforming-the-lives-of-smallholder-farmers-in-kenya-lessons-and-the-road-ahead/)

Cite both. Differentiate on unit (enterprise, not household), outcome (conversion, not earnings), and deliverable (a constraint ranking, not an association).

Also on gender and productive use: **Pueyo et al. (2020), Tanzania** — men-owned enterprises use electricity more frequently and intensively, women dominate productive use of *cooking fuels*, and absent gender interventions "male entrepreneurs are more likely to benefit from the promotion of productive uses of energy." → [paper](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c8922341a0ba50acaa7f0637bf0b456a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
